# From Smoke to Qubits: A Short History of Communication

Human history is also a history of **defeating distance**. From a bonfire
on a hilltop to a screen in our palm, we have spent three thousand years
trying to deliver one sentence farther, faster and more faithfully.

## Beacons and Post Horses

In the late Western Zhou dynasty, beacon towers stood on the ridge of Mount Li,
one after another: smoke by day, fire by night. When one tower lit up,
its neighbours answered, and news of invasion crossed a thousand li of
mountains in a single night. It was the fastest messenger of the agricultural
age — it carried no details, only **one single bit**: the enemy is coming.

Beacons could raise alarms, but they could not carry a family letter.
So came the post relay: a station every thirty li, documents passed
from rider to rider. By the Tang dynasty more than sixteen hundred stations
blanketed the empire, and urgent dispatches covered five hundred li a day.
An ordinary letter, entrusted to merchants and travellers, trembled on the
road for months. When Du Fu wrote that a letter home was worth ten thousand
gold, he was really describing **uncertainty** itself.

## The Awakening of Electricity

In May 1844, between Washington and Baltimore, Samuel Morse tapped out a
sentence: What hath God wrought. Current raced down a copper wire and
arrived forty miles away instantly. For the first time, information moved
faster than a horse, than the wind, than sound itself.

The telegraph reset the tempo of war, of railways and of newspapers.
Laying a cable across the Atlantic took ten years, five failures and
a fortune — and on the night it finally worked in 1866, cities on both
sides rang their bells, because two continents now **shared the same moment**.

## Printing, Radio and War

Before electricity there had been a quieter revolution: Gutenberg's movable
type. A book that once cost a monk years of copying could now be multiplied
by the thousand overnight. Knowledge acquired **scale**, and literacy,
science and public opinion all stood on that foundation.

Maxwell predicted electromagnetic waves with four equations, Hertz proved
they existed, and Marconi threw them across the Atlantic. Radio needed no
wire; the air itself filled with signals, and with the battlefields of the
twentieth century. When the Titanic sank, her wireless distress call saved
seven hundred lives, and nations wrote laws forcing ships to keep watch
around the clock.

## Satellites and Glass

In 1957 a polished aluminium sphere, fifty-eight centimetres wide, swept
across the night sky, and radio amateurs heard its beep-beep from orbit.
Within a decade humanity had pointed its antennas upward: live
transcontinental television, overseas calls, weather charts — and the first
**complete photograph of the Earth**.

Almost at the same time, Charles Kao proposed in a paper that if you draw
glass into threads thin as hair, light would travel inside them even when
they bend. Academies laughed. Today undersea fibre carries more than
ninety percent of all intercontinental data, and a single fibre talks
more calls than the whole copper cable of a century ago.

## The Connected Age

The ARPANET of 1969 had four nodes. On its first attempt, scientists tried
to send the word LOGIN; the system crashed after two letters. That was the
first message of the internet: LO.

From hypertext to browsers, from electronic mail to instant messaging,
the network stitched every computer on Earth into one machine. The cost of
copying information fell to nearly zero, and **distance dissolved in the
digital world**. We no longer write letters; we watch the words
"the other person is typing".

## Qubits and What Comes Next

Today engineers attempt what once seemed unthinkable: using quantum
entanglement and the no-cloning theorem to build keys that cannot be
wiretapped in principle. The Micius satellite has distributed entangled
photons to ground stations a thousand kilometres apart, proving again and
again that nature permits us to keep secrets in the strangest possible way.

From wolf smoke to glass fibre, from post horses to photons, the story
repeats one lesson: every technology that abolishes distance ends up
redefining the relations between people. The first line of the next three
thousand years is perhaps being written, right now, in some quiet laboratory.
